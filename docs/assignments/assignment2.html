Assignment 2: Who Do We Know the Least About?

Measuring uncertainty in American Community Survey data

Setup

library(tidyverse)
library(tidycensus)

The question

Every number the American Community Survey publishes is an estimate drawn from a sample, not a count. That means every number comes with a margin of error — a statement about how wrong it might be.

Most people ignore it. In this assignment, you won’t.

In this analysis, I look at Median Home Value across counties in Illinois, and ask which communities we measure most and least reliably.

Choosing what to measure

my_state <- "IL"        # two-letter abbreviation, in quotes
my_variable <- "B25077_001"     # the variable code, in quotes

Pulling the data

county_data <- get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = my_variable,
  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5"
)

Let’s look at what came back.

How big is the margin of error?

A margin of error of 3,000 means something very different for an estimate of 20,000 than for an estimate of 200,000. To compare across places, we need it as a percentage.

county_data <- county_data %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = moe / estimate * 100)

Now sort to see which counties are measured least reliably.

county_data %>%
  arrange(desc(moe_pct)) %>%
  select(NAME, estimate, moe, moe_pct) %>%
  head(10)
# A tibble: 10 × 4

Hardin County is the county with the largest margin of error relative to its estimate at 34.09924.

Sorting counties into reliability categories

county_data <- county_data %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 5  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 10 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  ))

count(county_data, reliability)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  reliability         n
  <chr>           <int>
1 High confidence    49
2 Low confidence     11
3 Moderate           42

From the calculations, 49 counties fall under ‘High Confidence’, 42 counties fall under ‘Moderate’, and 11 counties fall under ‘Low Confidence’.

Is the margin of error bigger in small places?

To answer this we need population and our variable side by side, in the same row. That means a wide pull.

county_wide <- get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = c(myvar = my_variable,
                pop   = "B01003_001"),
  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5",
  output = "wide"
)

head(county_wide)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  GEOID NAME                       myvarE myvarM  popE  popM
  <chr> <chr>                       <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1 17001 Adams County, Illinois     155900   4554 65166    NA
2 17003 Alexander County, Illinois  58700  10578  5042    NA
3 17005 Bond County, Illinois      133100  13683 16627    NA
4 17007 Boone County, Illinois     198600   8350 53316    NA
5 17009 Brown County, Illinois     142200  19435  6320    NA
6 17011 Bureau County, Illinois    121900   3733 33027    NA
county_wide <- county_wide %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = myvarM / myvarE * 100) %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 5  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 10 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  ))

Now compare the average population of counties in each reliability category.

county_wide %>%
  group_by(reliability) %>%
  summarize(
    n_counties = n(),
    avg_population = mean(popE)
  )
# A tibble: 3 × 3
  reliability     n_counties avg_population
  <chr>                <int>          <dbl>
1 High confidence         49        240766.
2 Low confidence          11          7351.
3 Moderate                42         19387

This table illustrates a pattern between average population and the level of confidence we have in the Median Home Value estimates. The average population of counties with high confidence was 240,766.12, while the average population of counties with low confidence was 7350.818. Counties with moderate levels of confidence had an average population of 19,387. The pattern is generally that with higher populations, the level of confidence in the data increases.

What this means for policy

Address all three:

  1. Which communities in your state do we know the least about? We know the least about the 11 counties with the lowest confidence levels. This would be Pope County, Bond County, Scott County, Massac County, Pulaski County, Calhoun County, Brown County, Hamilton County, Jasper County, Alexander County, and Hardin County, which had a margin of error percent range of 10.275 to 34.099. As seen in the above table, these counties also have lower average populations compared to the rest of the state.
  2. Why? Connect the pattern to how the ACS actually works — it’s a sample, and smaller places yield smaller samples. As the ACS5 already measures a slice of the county population, smaller counties like Hardin County (with about a population of 3623) will have smaller samples that lead to higher variability in the median. With a small population, the statistical standard error increases, resulting in imprecision within the data. Hence, we can conclude that the states with the lower populations are the communities that we know the least about from the ACS5 data.
  3. What could go wrong? Imagine a state agency allocating funding using your variable, treating every county’s number as equally certain. Who is likely to be hurt, and how? If a state agency allocates funding using the variable of median home values, there is a high probability that the ACS5 estimate of the true median of a county may be higher than the actual value. This error in the estimate may result in low confidence counties appearing wealthier than they actually are and receiving less aid than they would need, thus resulting in an inequitable provision of welfare funding.
